THE STUDY BEHIND THE GAME

Research notes

The same choices do not carry the same meaning in every telling. This game uses three interpretive lenses, each tied to specific story details.

1697 — Charles Perrault

Red brings cakes and a pot of butter, gives away her grandmother's location, and follows the longer route, gathering nuts, chasing butterflies, and picking flowers. Her mother does not explicitly forbid leaving the path. The tale ends without a rescue and appends a warning about charming wolves and misplaced trust.

“Little Red Riding Hood,” in The Classic Fairy Tales, ed. Maria Tatar (Norton, 1999), pp. 11–13.

1857 — Jacob & Wilhelm Grimm

Red carries cake and wine. Her mother tells her not to leave the path, to mind her manners, and to say good morning. After the wolf distracts her with flowers and birds, a huntsman cuts both victims out of the wolf and Red helps fill its belly with stones. In a second encounter, Red and her grandmother foil another wolf with sausage water and a trough.

Rotkäppchen, Kinder- und Hausmärchen, vol. 1 (1857), pp. 140–44; trans. D. L. Ashliman.

20th-century parodies — A new kind of Red

James Thurber's “The Little Girl and the Wolf” (1940) and Roald Dahl's “Little Red Riding Hood and the Wolf” (1982) depict a Red who recognizes or defeats the wolf herself. This game's final chapter is an original, nonviolent interpretation inspired by those role reversals, not a scene copied from either text.

James Thurber, Fables for Our Time (1940), pp. 5–6; Roald Dahl, Revolting Rhymes (1982).

The research argument is deliberately open to debate: is a “good” child obedient, cautious, teachable, or capable of acting independently? Your choices make that question playable.
