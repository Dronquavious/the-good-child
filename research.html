Research and artist’s statement

How do different versions of Little Red Riding Hood use obedience and disobedience to define what makes a child “good”?

Thesis

Perrault locates danger in misplaced trust, the Grimms give obedience an explicit rule and make room for learning through experience, and Thurber and Dahl give Red the power to recognize and confront danger herself. Repeating the same three encounters lets players see how the storyteller’s expectations change the meaning of a child’s choices.

Artist’s statement

I built The Good Child around three moments Red faces in every chapter. She meets the wolf, chooses a route, and decides what to do at Grandmother’s door. I kept those moments in the same order so the player could feel the rules change around a familiar story. In Perrault, the longer road matters because the wolf takes a shortcut. In Grimm, leaving the path also breaks a rule Red promised to follow.

Zohar Shavit’s work on changing ideas of childhood helped me ask what each telling expects a child to know. Maria Tatar’s study of fear and discipline helped me read the danger alongside the moral each version teaches. Sandra Beckett’s work on retellings helped me see how familiar scenes become opportunities to give Red a different role.

I gave the modern Red a way to question the disguise and step back. That nonviolent choice is my invention, inspired by the role reversals in Thurber and Dahl. Each chapter pairs the player’s what-if ending with the published one so readers can compare who acts, who helps, and who gets another chance.
